## Verified core facts (primary source)
- On 25 November 2026 the FCC granted SpaceX conditional authorization for up to
  1,000,000 satellites in a constellation called **Starmind**, designed as orbital data
  centres that process data in space and send results back to Earth.
- The FCC called it the first US authorization of a constellation of data centres in space.
- Primary source: FCC press release, "FCC Authorizes 1 Million Satellites for Data
  Centers in Space", 25 Nov 2026, docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-427572A1.pdf.
  The underlying order is DA 26-1273, adopted and released 25 Nov 2026 by the FCC
  Space Bureau.
- The FCC release explicitly says the system "operates consistent with the FCC's orbital
  debris rules" for the approved shells, and that the agency continues to review other
  orbital data centre proposals.
-The authorization covers radiofrequency links: backup and emergency TT&C, and optical inter-satellite
  links supplemented by Ka-band downlink 18.8-19.3 GHz and uplink 28.6-29.1 GHz.
- Chairman Brendan Carr framed it as US leadership in "super intelligence"; Musk thanked
  him on X.

## What the StudyIQ video adds (verified from its Hindi transcript)
The video is a UPSC-focused explainer by Ankit Agrawal (StudyIQ IAS), published
26 Nov 2026. Its description is a copy-paste error about Bangladesh, but the spoken
content is on topic and matches the primary sources. Use these points as the
article's explanatory backbone:
- Scale context: roughly 20,000 satellites sent to date, about 16,000-17,000 active.
- Starmind satellites are **orbital computing modules** with GPUs and processors,
  laser-linked to each other, with solar arrays for power; results reach Earth via
  Starlink. Starlink transmits internet data; Starmind does the computing. They are
  interdependent but different systems.
- Three claimed advantages of putting data centers in space: near-continuous solar
  power, less dependence on terrestrial grids and transmission lines, and heat
  rejection by radiation rather than water cooling. The video itself cautions that
  cooling is not simple.
- Conventional data centers consume large amounts of electricity and water for
  cooling, need land, and create environmental concerns.
- Timeline as the video states it: first deployment November 2033, 100,000 by
  November 2035, 500,000 by November 2038, 1 million by 2040. It stresses these are
  targets and the final number could be 500,000 or 1.5 million.
- Reusable **Starship** is key to launching heavy computing satellites affordably.
- Risks the video explains well: space debris and **Kessler syndrome** (a collision
  chain reaction), radiation damage to memory and semiconductors, cooling in a
  vacuum through specialized radiators, impossible repair and maintenance in orbit,
  and launch/manufacturing cost.
- Geopolitical frame: a US-China-Russia race for technological supremacy spanning
  semiconductors, AI modules, 5G and outer space.
- The video ends with a question about the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. That is a good
  closing FAQ angle, but verify the treaty text from an official source before use.

## Important nuances (must be in the article, not buried)
- **It is a conditional, partial grant, not a blank cheque.** The FCC granted in part and
  deferred in part. SpaceX must file a final orbital-debris plan before deploying any
  Starmind satellite above 600 km. Its request to dispose of higher satellites into Earth
  disposal or heliocentric orbits instead of atmospheric reentry was **not** granted;
  the FCC said it had not received enough information.
- **Deployment deadlines:** reporting on the order gives four milestones: first satellite
  operating by 25 Nov 2033, 10% by 25 Nov 2035, 50% by 25 Nov 2038 and the full
  constellation by 25 Nov 2040. The licence can become void if milestones are missed.
  These deadlines should be attributed to the order and checked against the PDF before
  publication.
- **Objections were filed and rejected:** Kuiper Systems (Amazon Leo), Viasat and the
  American Astronomical Society petitioned to deny; the FCC denied all three. Concerns
  included orbital debris, collision risk, radiofrequency interference and effects on
  astronomy. The FCC said several concerns fell outside its regulatory authority.
- **Scale is aspirational.** Operators often request more satellites than they fly.
  Reuters notes SpaceX once requested 42,000 Starlink satellites and roughly 11,000 are
  in orbit today; about 17,000 satellites of all kinds are in orbit. Do not present
  1,000,000 as a near-term reality.
- **Technical and economic obstacles remain:** heat rejection/cooling in space, launch
  and manufacturing cost, financing, and proximity operations. These are reported by
  TechCrunch and Spaceflight Now, not yet established fact.
